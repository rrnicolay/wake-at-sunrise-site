Sunrise alarm apps vs sunrise lamps

November 18, 2026 · Português

Search for "sunrise alarm" and you will find two very different things under the same name. One kind makes light that imitates dawn at a time you choose. The other kind rings at the time the real sun rises where you are. They solve different problems, and they can work together.

Dawn simulators: a sunrise at the time you pick

A sunrise lamp, also called a wake-up light or dawn simulator, brightens slowly in the minutes before your alarm. The Philips Wake-up Light, for example, reaches up to 300 lux at 40 to 50 cm and runs its sunrise over about 20 to 40 minutes, depending on the model (Philips). Many phone apps do the same with the screen, and some control smart bulbs.

Research on dawn simulation is encouraging. In a Dutch study, an artificial dawn of 50 or 250 lux reduced complaints of grogginess on waking (sleep inertia) over two weeks, although it did not shift participants' melatonin rhythm (Gimenez et al., 2010). In people with seasonal depression, a 90-minute dawn signal peaking at 250 lux led to more remissions than a placebo light (Avery et al., 2001).

Phone screens are a weak dawn

Those studies used lamps. We found no study testing whether a phone screen glowing on a bedside table does the same, and there is a reason to doubt it: closed eyelids block most light. One classic measurement found that eyelids let through about 0.3 percent of blue and green light and about 5.6 percent of red (Ando and Kripke, 1996). A small screen, facing away or across the room, has little light to spare. A screen-glow app can still make a gentle wake-up routine; just do not expect it to act like a 300-lux lamp.

Real-sunrise alarms: the actual sun, on schedule

A real-sunrise alarm does not make light. It computes when the sun rises at your location and rings then, or at an offset you choose. The light comes from outside, and it is free and as bright as the day allows. Its strength is timing: your wake-up follows the seasons on its own, so you are up when daylight arrives, and you can open the curtains or step out into it.

Its limit is the season. At sunrise itself the sky is only starting to brighten, and in a northern winter the sun can rise after you need to be at work: around 8:15 in Berlin and 8:45 in Stockholm at the end of December. On those days a real-sunrise alarm either rings late or, with an offset before sunrise, in the dark.

Using both

They combine well. In summer and around the equinoxes, a real-sunrise alarm lets the morning do the work. In the darkest months, a lamp can add the light the sky does not yet have. Some smart-home systems can start a lamp at sunrise with an offset, which brings the two ideas together.
