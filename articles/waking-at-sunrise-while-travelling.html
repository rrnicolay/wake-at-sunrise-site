Waking at sunrise while travelling

November 18, 2026 · Português

Travelling changes your mornings twice over: the clock on your phone may change, and the sun itself rises at a different time. If you like waking at sunrise, it helps to know which of the two you are dealing with, and how to use morning light to settle in faster.

Sunrise follows the place, not the time zone

Time zones are wide, and sunrise moves by about four minutes for every degree of longitude. So even without crossing a time zone, the sun can rise at a noticeably different clock time. A few examples for 2026, computed with NOAA's solar equations:

  • Germany: on 21 June the sun rises at 04:44 in Görlitz, on the Polish border, and at 05:22 in Aachen, on the Belgian border. Same clock, 38 minutes apart.
  • Spain: on 18 November, sunrise is at 07:43 in Barcelona and at 08:30 in A Coruña. Spain uses Central European Time even though most of it lies west of London.
  • Brazil: Recife and Brasília share the same time. On 18 November the sun rises at 04:49 in Recife and at 05:30 in Brasília.

A fixed alarm ignores all of this. An alarm tied to sunrise moves with you.

Going north or south: same clock, different season of light

Some trips change sunrise by hours without much jet lag. Fly from São Paulo to Lisbon in December and you move from a 05:16 sunrise to a 07:50 one, with only three hours of time difference. Drive from Berlin to Stockholm in June and sunrise moves from 04:43 to 03:30. Your body clock is not confused by the trip itself, but the light arrives at a very different moment.

Crossing time zones: use morning light on purpose

Jet lag happens because your internal clock keeps home time for a while after you land. Light is its strongest signal. According to the CDC's guidance for travellers, bright light in the morning, after the low point of your internal night, moves the body clock earlier, and light in the evening moves it later. People adapt by roughly one hour a day after flying east and about an hour and a half a day after flying west (CDC Yellow Book, Jet lag disorder).

  • After flying east (for example from Brazil to Europe), morning light at your destination helps you shift earlier. Getting up around the local sunrise and going outside is a simple way to get it.
  • After a long eastward flight, the destination's early morning can still fall in the middle of your internal night. Light at that point pushes the clock the wrong way, which is why the CDC's eastward example tells travellers to avoid bright early light at first. For the first day or two, a later wake-up, such as an hour after sunrise, can work better.
  • After flying west, you tend to wake too early. Afternoon and evening light helps more than dawn light, so an offset after sunrise is kinder than waking at first light.

Practical tips

  • Check the new sunrise time before you sleep on the first night. It may be very different from home.
  • Open the curtains or step outside within the first hour after waking.
  • Keep your usual offset from sunrise if you have one, so the relationship between light and waking stays the same.
  • In the far north in winter, sunrise can come late in the morning. If you must be up earlier, use an alarm offset before sunrise and bright indoor light.

How Wake at Sunrise handles a trip

The app computes sunrise from your approximate location on the phone, and stores each sunrise as an absolute moment, so a time zone change is shown correctly on its own. It picks up a new location when you open the app, and when an alarm rings. So after you land, open it once and the next alarm moves to the local sunrise. On Android it can also use the location the phone already has from other apps while it runs in the background. Nothing is sent anywhere: the location stays on the device.
