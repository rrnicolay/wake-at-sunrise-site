Why waking up with the sun works, and why it is harder than it used to be

November 16, 2026 · Português

For most of human history, people woke up with the light. Today most of us wake up to a fixed alarm, in a dark room, and spend the morning indoors. The body has noticed. Here is what morning light actually does, why modern life weakens it, and how to get some of it back.

Your body runs on a clock that light sets

Inside the brain there is a circadian clock that runs on a cycle of roughly 24 hours. It decides when you feel sleepy, when you feel alert, and when your body releases melatonin, the hormone that prepares you for sleep. The clock is not perfectly accurate on its own, so it needs a daily reset, and its strongest signal is light reaching the eyes.

When that light arrives matters. Bright light in the morning pulls the clock earlier, which makes it easier to fall asleep at night and to wake up the next day. Light late in the evening pushes it later. In one laboratory study, a day spent in dim light let participants' clocks drift later, and a single hour of bright morning light the next day pulled them back (Ohashi et al., 2023).

Indoors, it is always dusk

Our eyes adapt so well that indoor rooms feel bright. To the body clock they are not. Light is measured in lux: a sunny day outside can reach 100,000 lux, and even an overcast sky gives thousands. A Danish study that measured people at work found that indoor workers received about 1,000 lux during summer working hours and only about 400 lux in winter, while outdoor workers received several times more (Aarhus University, 2019).

So a modern day often sends the clock a weak, flat signal: too little light in the morning, and plenty of screen and lamp light at night. The clock drifts later, and getting up at a fixed hour starts to feel like waking in the middle of the night.

What one week of camping showed

Researchers at the University of Colorado took eight people camping in the Rocky Mountains for a week, with no artificial light at all, only the sun and the campfire. Before the trip, their internal night started around two hours later than it did after it. By the end of the week, everyone's clock was synchronized to sunrise and sunset, early birds and night owls alike. Natural light during the trip was about four times brighter than the light they got in their normal lives (Wright et al., 2013).

The lesson is not that you need to move to a cabin. It is that the clock responds quickly when the light signal is strong and well timed.

And vitamin D?

Sunlight helps the skin make vitamin D, which is one more reason to spend time outdoors. But that comes from UVB, the part of sunlight that depends on the season and the time of day (Harvard Health), and UVB is weakest when the sun is low on the horizon. The light right after sunrise is excellent for the body clock and does little for vitamin D. Both are good reasons to go outside, at different times of day.

How to wake with the sun when you live indoors

  • Get outside in the first hour after waking, even for ten minutes. A short walk, coffee on the balcony or the commute on foot all count; a window helps less than open air.
  • Open the curtains as soon as you get up, and keep the brightest room for breakfast.
  • Dim the lights in the evening, so the clock is not pushed later at night.
  • Keep your wake-up time close to sunrise, so the first light of the day lands when you are actually awake to see it.
