How far sunrise moves through the year in Brazil, Germany, the UK and the Nordics

November 18, 2026 · Português

Sunrise is not a fixed time. It moves a little every day, and how much it moves over a year depends mostly on one thing: how far you live from the equator. Near the equator it barely changes. In Scandinavia it swings by more than five hours. Here is what that looks like in four places where people often wonder why their alarm feels so different in June and in December.

The European swings include the one-hour jump of daylight saving time. Without it, Berlin's sunrise would still move by about two and a half hours over the year.

Why latitude decides it

The Earth's axis is tilted by about 23.4 degrees. As the planet goes around the sun, each hemisphere leans towards the sun for half the year and away from it for the other half. At the equator that tilt hardly changes the length of the day. The further you go towards a pole, the more the day stretches in summer and shrinks in winter, and sunrise moves with it.

Brazil: a small swing, and no more clock changes

Most Brazilians live between the equator and about 30 degrees south, so the swing is modest: about half an hour in Manaus, an hour and a half in São Paulo, two hours in Porto Alegre. Since 2019 Brazil has had no daylight saving time (Decree 9,772/2019), so the clock-time numbers are the sun's own rhythm. That also means the summer sun rises very early: around 5:00 in Rio de Janeiro at the end of November. Waking right at sunrise is a choice for early risers; many people use an offset, such as 30 or 60 minutes after sunrise.

Germany and the UK: three to four hours

Berlin, Hamburg and London sit above 51 degrees north. In June the sun rises before 5:00; at the end of December not until after 8:00. On top of that, the clocks change twice a year under the EU's summer-time rules (Directive 2000/84/EC), which the UK also follows: on the last Sunday of October, sunrise jumps an hour earlier overnight, and on the last Sunday of March it jumps an hour later. Around the equinoxes, sunrise in Berlin moves by more than two minutes a day.

Scotland goes further. Edinburgh's sunrise ranges from about 4:25 to 8:45, and in Lerwick, on Shetland, from about 3:40 to 9:10.

The Nordics: more than five hours, and then none at all

Stockholm, Oslo and Helsinki are around 60 degrees north. Their sunrise moves by over five hours across the year, and around the equinoxes by about three minutes a day, so a week is enough to notice. North of the Arctic Circle the sun does not rise at all for part of the winter: in Tromsø, polar night lasts from about 27 November to 15 January, and the midnight sun shines from mid-May to late July (Visit Tromsø, Yr).

A curious detail: the earliest sunrise is not on the longest day

The longest day of the year is the June solstice, but in Berlin the earliest sunrise comes on 17 June and the latest on 30 December, not on 21 December. The cause is the equation of time: because the Earth's orbit is an ellipse and its axis is tilted, solar noon drifts against clock noon through the year, and that shifts sunrise and sunset in opposite directions around the solstices (FOX Weather).

What this means for a fixed alarm

Take a 6:30 alarm in Berlin. In June it rings almost two hours after sunrise, in full daylight. In late December it rings an hour and three quarters before sunrise, in the dark. The same alarm, two very different mornings. In São Paulo, the same 6:30 alarm ranges from over an hour after sunrise in November to about twenty minutes before it in July.

How these numbers were computed

The times come from the solar equations NOAA publishes for its Solar Calculator, based on Jean Meeus's Astronomical Algorithms (NOAA), for each city centre in 2026, with a sea-level horizon. NOAA states these results are accurate to within a minute between 72 degrees north and south. Published tables can differ by a minute because of rounding or slightly different coordinates. Hills, buildings and the weather change when you actually see the sun.
